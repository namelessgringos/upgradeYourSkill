# Strength Programming

## Progressive overload is the entire organising principle

If the work you do in the gym does not increase over time, your body has no reason to change. That is the whole idea, and everything else in this guide is a method for making it happen reliably.

"More work" has a specific meaning. It is more weight on the bar, more repetitions at the same weight, or more hard sets across the week. Those three levers are the only ones that matter for a beginner or intermediate lifter. Doing the same five sets of eight with the same dumbbells you used in March is exercise. It maintains what you have. It is not training, and it will not move you forward no matter how sweaty or sore it makes you.

The practical consequence is that you need a record. Not an app with graphs and streaks — a notes file or a cheap paper notebook with the lift, the weight, the sets, and the reps. Without it you cannot tell whether this week beat last week, and if you cannot tell, you will drift into repeating whatever felt comfortable. Almost every person who trains for a year without progress is repeating a workout they memorised, and they do not know it because they never wrote anything down.

Overload also has to be small. The mistake in the other direction is adding fifteen kilos because you felt strong, missing your reps, and concluding you are stuck. Progress in a trained adult is measured in single reps and the smallest plates in the rack. A squat that goes up 2.5 kg a week for six months has gone up 60 kg. Almost nobody achieves that, because almost nobody is patient enough to add only 2.5 kg.

One more definition, because it appears throughout this guide. A "hard set" means a set taken close enough to failure that you could have done roughly one to four more reps, no more. Sets stopped well short of that count for very little. When this guide says ten sets a week, it means ten sets that were genuinely hard, not twenty sets of which half were warm-ups you counted out of optimism.

## Choose your lifts once and then leave them alone

You need one main movement for each of five patterns: a squat, a hinge, a horizontal push, a horizontal pull, and a vertical push or pull. That is it. Everything else is an accessory that supports one of those.

For most people the defaults are a barbell back squat or goblet squat, a Romanian deadlift or conventional deadlift, a bench press or dumbbell press, a row, and an overhead press or a chin-up. If you train at home with dumbbells, substitute freely: a dumbbell split squat is a fine squat, a dumbbell RDL is a fine hinge. The specific exercise matters far less than picking one you can load progressively and can perform consistently without pain.

Once chosen, keep them for at least twelve weeks. This is the part busy people get wrong most often. Rotating exercises every few weeks feels productive and destroys your ability to see progress, because a new lift always feels harder and you have no history to compare against. Novelty is not a training stimulus. Load is.

Accessories are where you do get variety, and they exist to cover what the main lifts miss or to fix a weak point. Two or three per session is plenty: some direct arm work, some direct hamstring or calf work, some side or rear delt work, some abdominal work if you like it. Accessories should be run in the 8-15 rep range and taken close to failure, because there is no reason to grind heavy triples on a lateral raise.

Rep ranges for the main lifts: 3-6 reps builds strength most efficiently, 6-12 builds hypertrophy — muscle size — most efficiently, and both ranges do a reasonable amount of the other thing. If you are a general trainee with no competition to prepare for, put your main lifts in the 4-8 range and your accessories in the 8-15 range and stop thinking about it. The differences between these ranges are real but small compared to the difference between progressing and not progressing.

## How much weekly volume, and how to change it

Volume is the number of hard sets per muscle group per week. The useful range for most people is 10 to 20 sets. Below about 10 you tend to maintain rather than grow. Above about 20, results flatten out for most people and recovery becomes the limiting factor rather than effort.

Start at the bottom. Ten hard sets per muscle group per week, run properly with a progression scheme, will grow almost any untrained or detrained adult for months. Starting at 20 sets leaves you nowhere to go when progress slows, and the only remaining lever is more time in the gym you do not have.

Count sets by what they train, not by what they are called. A bench press set counts as a chest set and roughly half a triceps set. A row counts as a back set and roughly half a biceps set. A squat counts for quads and something for glutes. Do not be forensic about this — you are trying to keep in a range, not calculate a dose.

Add volume when three conditions hold at once: your lifts are still going up, you are not accumulating soreness that lasts more than about 48 hours, and you are sleeping and eating roughly normally. When all three are true, add one or two sets per muscle group and hold there for a few weeks. When any of them stops being true, hold volume where it is or cut it.

The signs you are doing too much are specific and worth memorising: your warm-up weights start feeling heavy, your sleep gets worse rather than better, joints ache in the morning, motivation to train drops off a cliff, and your top sets go down two weeks in a row while nothing else in your life has changed. One bad session is noise. Two bad weeks is data.

## Structuring a week around a schedule you actually have

Pick the number of training days you will still hit in week nine, when work is bad and you are tired. For most working adults that number is three. Not four, not five. Three sessions of 45 to 60 minutes.

Full-body three times a week is the highest-leverage template for that constraint, for one specific reason: every muscle gets three stimulating sessions a week, and missing one day costs you a third of the week rather than everything you were going to do for your chest. On a body-part split where chest lives only on Monday, a bad Monday means your chest gets nothing for seven days.

Here is a full example week for someone training Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, using a barbell and dumbbells.

**Monday.** Back squat, 3 sets of 5. Bench press, 3 sets of 6. Barbell row, 3 sets of 8. Romanian deadlift, 2 sets of 10. Dumbbell curl, 2 sets of 12. Roughly 50 minutes with 2-3 minutes of rest between the heavy sets.

**Wednesday.** Romanian deadlift, 3 sets of 6. Overhead press, 3 sets of 6. Chin-up or lat pulldown, 3 sets of 8. Leg press or split squat, 2 sets of 10. Lateral raise, 2 sets of 15.

**Friday.** Front squat or goblet squat, 3 sets of 6. Incline dumbbell press, 3 sets of 8. Cable row or dumbbell row, 3 sets of 10. Leg curl, 2 sets of 12. Triceps extension, 2 sets of 12.

Count the chest sets: three on Monday, three on Wednesday from the overhead press counting partially, three on Friday. That lands in the low end of the useful range, which is exactly where you want to start. The same arithmetic works for back and legs.

If you have four days, the next step up is an upper/lower split: upper body Monday and Thursday, lower body Tuesday and Friday. Do not go to four days because five would be better in theory. Go to four days when three has felt easy for a month and your schedule genuinely supports it.

Session order matters within a day. Do the heaviest, most technical lift first, when you are fresh. Do accessories last, when fatigue affects them least. Rest 2 to 3 minutes between hard sets of main lifts and 60 to 90 seconds on accessories. Short rest periods do not make training more efficient; they make your sets weaker, which reduces the overload that is the entire point.

## A progression scheme you can run for months

Use double progression. You progress reps first inside a range, then weight, then start again at the bottom of the range. It is the most robust scheme for someone who is not on a rigid schedule, because it does not punish you for a bad week.

Set a rep range for each main lift — say 5 to 8 for the bench press — and a number of sets, say three. The rule is: when you hit the top of the range on every set in a session, add the smallest available increment next session and drop back to the bottom of the range.

A worked example, bench pressing 60 kg for 3 sets in a 5-8 range.

Week 1: 60 kg for 6, 5, 5. Not at the top on all sets, so the weight stays.
Week 2: 60 kg for 7, 6, 6. Still not there. Weight stays.
Week 3: 60 kg for 8, 7, 7. Closer. Weight stays.
Week 4: 60 kg for 8, 8, 8. Top of the range on every set. Next session you go to 62.5 kg.
Week 5: 62.5 kg for 6, 5, 5. You have restarted the cycle two and a half kilos heavier, and you will climb again.

That is four weeks for 2.5 kg on one lift, which sounds slow until you notice it is 30 kg a year on a lift you had stalled on entirely. Upper body lifts progress about half as fast as lower body ones, so expect squats and deadlifts to move roughly twice as quickly.

Two rules keep this honest. First, add the smallest increment available, which means buying 1.25 kg plates or 0.5 kg microplates if your gym's smallest jump is 5 kg on a bench press. Second, the reps must be real reps with the same technique you used last week. Half-reps at a heavier weight are not progress; they are a change of exercise.

If you fail to hit the bottom of the rep range at a given weight in two consecutive sessions, drop the weight by about 10 percent and climb back up. That is not a setback. It is the mechanism working.

## Autoregulation and what to do on a bad day

RPE means rate of perceived exertion — a 1-to-10 rating of how hard a set was, where 10 means you could not have done another rep, 9 means you had one left, and 8 means you had two left. Most of your main-lift work should sit at RPE 7 to 9. Most accessory work should sit at 8 to 9.

RPE is useful because your capacity on any given day is not fixed. Sleep, stress, food, and whatever happened at work all move it. A plan written on paper assumes today is average. Some days are not.

So on a day when the warm-up feels wrong and your working weight moves like it is 10 kg heavier, do not grind through the prescribed session, and do not skip it. Do this instead. Take 10 percent off the bar and hit your prescribed sets and reps at that lighter weight. You keep the movement, you keep the habit, you accumulate real volume, and you do not dig a hole you spend a week climbing out of. Log it as a lighter day and go back to your normal weight next session.

If two lighter days in a row still feel terrible, that is not a bad day, it is accumulated fatigue, and the answer is the next section.

Note the difference between a hard day and a warning sign. Feeling weak and unmotivated is a hard day. Sharp, localised, or unfamiliar pain during a specific movement is not something to autoregulate around — stop that movement. Muscle soreness that arrives a day later, is spread across a whole muscle, and eases as you warm up is normal. Pain that is sharp, joint-centred, present at rest, or getting worse across sessions needs a clinician, not a lighter set.

## Deloads and managing fatigue before it manages you

Fatigue accumulates faster than you notice, because it masks fitness. You get fitter and more tired at the same time, and the tiredness hides the fitness. A deload removes the tiredness so the fitness shows up.

Deload every 4 to 8 weeks, before you need it. The practical version: keep your exercises and your weights, and cut your number of sets by roughly 40 percent for one week. Three sets of five becomes two sets of five. Skip the accessories you like least. Do not go to failure on anything.

The alternative version, if your joints rather than your energy are the problem, is to keep the sets and cut the weight by about 10 percent for a week. Either works. What does not work is taking a full week off the gym entirely, because for most people that week becomes three.

The signal that you needed a deload two weeks ago: your top set weights are drifting down, warm-ups feel heavy, you are sleeping badly, and you are finding excuses. Deloading on a schedule rather than on symptoms avoids all of that.

Recovery outside the gym is not optional and there are only three things that matter. Sleep in the range of seven to nine hours; nothing in your program can compensate for six. Eat enough total food and enough protein — a reasonable working figure is 1.6 to 2.2 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day. Walk. If your job is sedentary and your only movement is three gym sessions, general activity does more for your recovery than any supplement, and that is as far into nutrition as this guide goes.

## Plateaus: what is actually causing them

A plateau almost never means you have reached your genetic limit. In an adult training under two years, it means one of five things, and they are worth checking in this order.

**You are not actually progressing the load.** Check your log. If you have run the same weight for six weeks and called it "consolidating", that is the plateau. Fix: run the double progression scheme above, strictly.

**You are under-recovering.** Sleep dropped, work got hard, you are eating less because you are busy. Fix: deload one week, then resume at the same weights. If the weights move again, that was it.

**Your jumps are too big.** You are trying to add 5 kg to a bench press every week and failing, then repeating the failure. Fix: 1.25 kg increments, or add reps before weight.

**Your volume is too low for your current level.** What grew you at 10 sets a week may not at month eight. Fix: add two sets per week to the stalled muscle group and hold for four weeks.

**Technique broke down as the weight climbed.** The bar path changed, the depth shrank, the range of motion quietly reduced. Fix: film one set from the side. This is the cause people are least willing to check and it is common.

Notice that four of the five fixes involve doing something different with the same exercises, and none of them involve a new program. The most common response to a plateau — abandoning the program for a different one — resets your progression and hides the actual cause.

## Keeping it going when life interrupts

Your program will be interrupted. Assume it and decide in advance what you do, because decisions made while tired and behind schedule are bad ones.

**You missed one session.** Do nothing. Do not double up, do not add it to the next day. Run the next scheduled session as written. A three-day week that becomes a two-day week roughly once a month costs you almost nothing.

**You missed a whole week.** Come back at the same weights, not lighter, but drop one set from each exercise for the first session. You have lost essentially no strength in seven days; you have lost some tolerance for volume.

**You missed three or four weeks.** Come back at about 90 percent of your previous weights and half the usual number of sets for one week, then rebuild over two to three weeks. You will be back where you were faster than you expect — regaining lost strength is much quicker than building it the first time.

**Your schedule permanently changed.** Rewrite the program down, not sideways. Three days becomes two full-body days, not three rushed ones. Two days of full-body training with four to six hard sets per muscle group each session maintains almost everything and can still add strength. Being consistent at a smaller program is worth more than being intermittent at a larger one, and this is the single decision that separates people still lifting in five years from people who quit twice a year.

Set the floor before you need it: the minimum session you will do on your worst week. Something like squat 2 sets, press 2 sets, row 2 sets, twenty-five minutes, done. On weeks when the full plan is impossible, you run the floor. The point is not the training effect of that session. The point is that you never stop, so there is never a restart.
