# Negotiation & Deals

## Before anything else, find your walk-away number

Most people prepare for a negotiation by deciding what they want. That is the wrong first question. The first question is what you will do if this conversation fails completely, because that answer, not your hopes, is what actually sets your power in the room.

Write down three numbers before you open your mouth.

The first is your walk-away: the number below which you would rather have no deal. Not the number that would annoy you. The number at which you genuinely say no and go do something else. For a salary conversation that might be "I stay in my current job." For a freelance project it might be "I keep the calendar space free for the retainer client I already have." Your walk-away is only real if the alternative behind it is real. If your alternative is "I panic and take it anyway," you do not have a walk-away, you have a wish, and the other side will find that out.

The second is your target: the number you are actually trying to land on. This should be grounded in something outside your own feelings. Job boards, published salary bands, what the last three comparable projects paid, what a peer told you over coffee, what the recruiter said the range was. You need one sentence of justification per number, and you need it ready.

The third is your opening ask, which sits above your target, because you will almost certainly move down and almost never move up.

Now do the part almost nobody does: write down what the other side wants. Not what they will pay. What problem is on their desk that you make go away. A hiring manager who has been interviewing for four months does not want a cheap hire, they want to stop interviewing. A client with a launch date in six weeks does not want the lowest rate, they want certainty that the thing ships. When you know what they are actually buying, you stop arguing about price and start arguing about value, which is a fight you can win.

One more piece of preparation: decide in advance what you will say if you are ambushed. "So, what are you looking for salary-wise?" arriving on a Tuesday call you thought was about scheduling is the single most common way people lose money. Have this memorized: "I want to give you a real answer rather than a number off the top of my head. Can I come back to you tomorrow?" Then come back tomorrow, prepared.

## Anchor first, anchor high, anchor defensible

Whoever puts the first credible number on the table shapes everything that follows. Every later number gets measured against it, including in the other person's head, and including when they think they are being objective. This is not a trick; it is how the conversation is scored.

The common advice is "never go first." That advice is for situations where you genuinely have no idea what the market is. If you have done the preparation above, go first.

The anchor has to be high enough to leave room and grounded enough to survive a follow-up question. A number with a reason attached is worth more than a "fair" number with none. Compare:

"I'm looking for around 95."

"I'm looking for 95. That reflects the range I've seen for this scope in this market, and it accounts for the fact that I'd be owning the integration work as well as the build."

The second one is harder to push back on, because pushing back now requires disagreeing with a stated reason rather than just saying "that's a lot."

Give one reason, not four. Stacking justifications reads as insecurity, and it hands them four things to attack instead of one. Say the number, say the reason, stop.

Anchor high, but stay inside the range where a reasonable person nods. An anchor so far outside the market that it damages your credibility costs you more than a modest one, because after that they stop treating your numbers as information. The test: could you defend this number to a friend in your field without wincing? If yes, say it.

If you are genuinely forced to let them go first, do not react to their number with your face or your voice. Repeat it back slowly as a question: "Eighty-two thousand." Then stop. That single repeated number, said flatly, does an enormous amount of work.

## The silence after the ask

You say your number. Then there is a pause. To you it feels like an accusation. To them it is four seconds of thinking.

This is the exact moment where most people give away money, and they do it with words like:

"...but I'm flexible."
"...obviously that's negotiable."
"...I mean, whatever works for your budget."

Every one of those sentences negotiates against yourself. You have just bid against your own number before the other side has said anything at all. You will never know what they would have said, because you did not let them say it.

The rule is simple and physically difficult: say the number, then stop talking. Not "pause for effect." Stop. If it helps, decide in advance what you will do with your mouth during the silence. Take a sip of water. Count to ten in your head. On a video call, this is the moment to look at the camera and say nothing at all.

What comes back is almost always one of four things: yes, a counter, a question, or "let me check." All four are better than the discount you were about to volunteer. Even a flat "that's too high" is useful information you were about to make unnecessary.

If the silence stretches past ten seconds and you truly must break it, break it with a question, never a concession: "What's your reaction to that?" or "Where does that sit against what you had in mind?" You have handed the next move back to them without moving your own number.

## A salary conversation, all the way through

Here is the whole thing, including the part where they say no.

You have prepared. Walk-away: 88, because you would stay where you are. Target: 100. Opening ask: 108.

**You:** "Thanks for putting the offer together. I'm genuinely interested in this role and I want to make it work. Before I say yes, I want to talk about the number. Based on the scope we discussed, and the fact that I'd be taking over the reporting workstream as well, I'm looking for 108."

**Them:** "That's well above the band we had in mind. We're at 92."

Do not flinch, and do not split the difference in your head yet. Ask a question.

**You:** "Help me understand the band. Is 92 the top of it, or the middle?"

**Them:** "It's near the top for this level."

**You:** "Okay. Then I think the question is the level, not the number. The role as described includes owning the reporting workstream, which in every org I've seen sits a level up. If we're calling it the level below, I understand 92. If I'm owning that work, I think we're looking at something that starts with a 1."

**Them:** "I hear you, but I don't have room to change the level right now."

This is the awkward middle. Nothing is agreed. Say the uncomfortable thing plainly.

**You:** "Then I want to be straight with you, because I'd rather you have real information than have me disappear. At 92 I don't move. That's not a tactic — it's just the maths against what I have now. At 100 I'm in, and I'd start on the date you asked for."

**Them:** "Let me see what I can do. I'm not promising 100."

**You:** "Understood. Take the time you need."

Then stop. Do not send a softening email that evening. Do not add "and honestly 96 would probably be fine." You have named a number you can defend and a floor you can live with, and the next move is theirs.

If they come back at 96, you have a real decision, not a panic. 96 is above your walk-away, so no answer is wrong. What you should not do is take 96 silently. Take it and buy something with it: "96 works if we put a review at six months with the level question on the table. Can you write that into the offer?"

## Trade, never concede

Every movement you make should buy something. This is the single habit that separates people who negotiate from people who get talked out of things politely.

The structure is always the same shape: "I can do X if Y."

"I can do that rate if we extend the contract to six months."
"I can hit that date if we cut the second report from the first release."
"I can come down to 95 if the bonus target is guaranteed for year one."
"I can start two weeks earlier if the signing bonus covers the notice period I'd be buying out."

Say the whole sentence as one sentence. If you say "I can do that rate" and then pause before the condition, you have already conceded and the condition sounds like an afterthought you can be argued out of.

Free concessions do not build goodwill. They teach the other side that pushing works, which guarantees more pushing. The person who drops 10% because the client sighed will be asked to drop another 10%, and will have taught them to sigh.

Keep a short list of things you can give that cost you little: start date flexibility, a longer contract, a testimonial or case study, payment in fewer instalments, dropping a nice-to-have deliverable, agreeing to a specific review date. These are your currency. Spend them deliberately, one at a time, each one bought with something you actually want.

## When they say the budget is fixed

"The budget is fixed" is usually true about the number and false about the deal. Treat it as information, not a wall. If the number cannot move, everything around the number can.

Here is a full rate conversation with a client, including the no.

**You:** "For the scope we've described — discovery, the build, two rounds of revisions, and handover — my price is 12,000."

**Them:** "We can't do 12. Our budget for this is 8, and it's fixed. It came from finance, I can't change it."

**You:** "Okay. I'll take that as real. Then let's make 8 buy the right thing rather than a worse version of everything. What's the piece you genuinely cannot launch without?"

**Them:** "The build. The discovery workshop is nice but we could live without it."

**You:** "Then here's what I can do at 8. Build, one round of revisions, handover, no discovery workshop, and the timeline moves out by two weeks so it fits alongside my other commitment. If you want the second revision round later, that's 1,500 as an add-on."

**Them:** "Two weeks later is tight for us. Can you keep the original date?"

**You:** "I can keep the original date if the deposit is 50% up front rather than on delivery — that lets me clear the calendar. Same 8, same scope."

**Them:** "I think we can do that."

Notice what happened. The price did not move, and neither did you. Scope moved, timing moved, payment terms moved, and a paid add-on appeared. Everyone can report a win to their boss, and you did not spend four days of unpaid work.

The question that unlocks this every time is: "What's the piece you genuinely cannot launch without?" It forces them to reveal priority, and priority is where the trade lives.

One test worth applying quietly: if the budget were genuinely fixed and small, would they have taken this meeting at all? Sometimes the honest answer is that you are the wrong supplier for this buyer. That is a fine outcome, and it is much better than discovering it three months in.

## Everything that is not the price

People fixate on the headline number because it is the easiest thing to compare. But most of the value in a deal, and most of the pain, lives in the terms nobody negotiated.

On a piece of client work, the terms that matter are: what exactly is in scope and what is explicitly out; how many revision rounds and what counts as a round; when you get paid and what triggers payment; what happens if their side goes quiet for three weeks; who owns what at the end; and what a change request costs. A written scope with an explicit "not included" list has saved more freelance relationships than any rate negotiation ever has.

On a job, the terms are: title and level; when the first review happens and what gets reviewed; remote and travel expectations in writing; notice period; what happens to the bonus if you join mid-year; who you report to. A six-month review with a specific agenda is worth real money and costs the company nothing today, which is exactly why it is often the easiest thing to win when the salary has genuinely stopped moving.

Ask for these in writing, cheerfully and without apology: "Can we get that into the offer letter? Not because I doubt you — just so it survives if you get promoted." That line works because it is true and it flatters them.

## What to do with a lowball

A lowball is a number so far under the market that it is not really an offer, it is a test of whether you know your own worth.

Do not get insulted, and do not counter immediately. An immediate counter anchors the whole negotiation to their number, which is what the lowball was for. Instead, refuse the frame.

"That's a fair bit below where this conversation needs to start. Can you tell me how you arrived at it?"

Then stop talking, again. One of two things happens. They explain, and the explanation reveals what they think they are buying, which is usually less than what you were selling — a fixable misunderstanding. Or they cannot explain, in which case you have just demonstrated that the number was arbitrary, and arbitrary numbers move.

If they push you to counter first, restate your own anchor rather than meeting theirs: "I'm not going to counter against that number, because I don't think it reflects the scope. I've been clear about 108 and why. If there's a path to something in that range, I'm listening."

Know when to leave. A counterparty who lowballs, then applies time pressure, then reacts to a reasonable ask with irritation is telling you what working with them will be like. Walking away from a bad deal is not a failed negotiation. It is the negotiation working.

## Closing without souring the relationship

You almost always have to live with the person afterwards. The manager who approves your raise assigns your projects. The client who agreed to your rate writes your referral. So the close matters as much as the number.

Three habits do most of the work.

Confirm in writing, immediately and warmly. "Great — confirming what we agreed: 100, start date the 14th, review at six months covering the level question. Anything I've got wrong, tell me and I'll fix it." This is not distrust. Memory is genuinely bad, and people change jobs.

Stop selling once you have won. When someone says yes, say "thank you, that's great" and move to logistics. Continuing to justify your number after agreement makes them wonder whether they overpaid, and reopens a settled question.

Never celebrate at their expense. No "I would have taken less." No visible triumph. The right note is that you both solved a problem together: "I appreciate you working through that with me — it wasn't the easiest conversation and I'm glad we landed somewhere that works." Say it and mean it, because it is true.

And if the answer was ultimately no, close that well too. "I understand, and I appreciate you being straight with me. If the budget picture changes in six months, I'd be glad to pick this up again." Deals that die cleanly come back. Deals that die badly do not.
