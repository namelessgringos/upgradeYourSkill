## Start here: the things that mean call now

Most of this guide is about staying calm and staying home. This section is the
exception, and it comes first on purpose, because the cost of reading it late
is not the same as the cost of reading anything else late.

Call your maternity triage line — the number on the front of your maternity
notes — **straight away, at any hour, without waiting to see if it settles**,
if any of these happen:

- **The baby's movements change.** Fewer, weaker, or a pattern that is not
  theirs. Do not lie down and count for an hour first. Do not drink something
  cold and wait. Do not use a home doppler — a heartbeat you find yourself
  proves nothing about how the baby is coping, and false reassurance is exactly
  how this goes wrong.
- **Any bleeding**, at any point in pregnancy, however light.
- **Waters that are green, brown, or bloodstained.** Clear or pink is expected.
  Green or brown means meconium, which needs assessing now.
- **A severe headache, seeing spots or flashing lights, sudden swelling of your
  face or hands, pain under your ribs, or vomiting late in pregnancy.** These
  are the signs of pre-eclampsia and they are the reason your blood pressure
  gets checked so often.
- **A fever**, or feeling shivery and unwell.
- **Pain that is constant** rather than coming and going in waves.

After the birth, the same rule applies to these:

- Soaking a maternity pad in an hour or less, or passing clots bigger than a
  golf ball.
- Pain, redness or swelling in one calf, or chest pain or breathlessness —
  these need **999**, not a callback.
- Feeling feverish, or discharge that smells offensive.
- Any thought of harming yourself or the baby. This is a medical emergency and
  it is treatable. Ring your GP for a same-day appointment, or 999 in crisis.
  Samaritans are on 116 123, free, at any hour.

And for the baby, in the first weeks:

- A temperature of 38°C or above under three months old.
- Difficulty breathing, grunting with each breath, or blue lips.
- Floppiness, or being unusually hard to wake.
- A rash that does not fade when you press a glass against it.
- Yellow skin in the first 24 hours of life.
- Refusing feeds, or a sharp drop in wet nappies.

Nobody will mind you calling. Triage midwives would far rather talk to a
hundred parents whose babies are fine than miss the one who is not. You are not
wasting anyone's time, and you do not need to apologise for ringing.

## Is this actually labour?

Labour rarely announces itself the way it does on television. For most first
babies it starts slowly and ambiguously, and the honest answer to "is this it?"
for several hours is "possibly".

**Braxton Hicks** are practice tightenings. They are irregular, they do not get
closer together, they usually ease if you change what you are doing, and they
are uncomfortable rather than painful. Walking around often stops them.

**Early — or latent — labour** is the long, unglamorous opening act. Cramping
that comes and goes, backache, a bloated or unsettled stomach, and often a
"show": a plug of thick mucus, sometimes streaked pink or with a little blood.
A show can appear days before anything else happens and, on its own, is not a
reason to go anywhere. The latent phase can last hours or, with a first baby,
more than a day. It is not a problem. It is the part everyone forgets to warn
you about.

**Established labour** is what the hospital is waiting for: contractions that
are regular, painful, and doing work. The rule of thumb used across the NHS is
**three in ten minutes, each lasting around sixty seconds, sustained for at
least an hour**. Time them from the *start* of one to the *start* of the next,
not from the end. Any phone timer app will do this; the partner's job is to run
it, so the labouring parent never has to watch a clock.

The test that beats any timer: during a real contraction, she cannot talk
through it. If she can hold a sentence to the end, it is early days yet.

**Waters breaking** happens to about one in ten people before contractions
start, and it is more often a trickle that keeps coming than a gush. Note the
time, the colour, and whether there is a smell. Put a pad on — not a tampon —
so you can see what is happening. Then call, whatever the colour, because the
plan changes once the waters have gone. Clear or faintly pink is the expected
finding. Green or brown is on the call-now list above.

## When to stay home, and when to go in

Hospitals send people home again in early labour, and being sent home when you
have psyched yourself up to arrive is demoralising. Staying home longer is
usually more comfortable, not less: your own bath, your own bed, your own
kitchen, nobody asking you questions between contractions.

Ring triage, rather than setting off, when contractions reach the three-in-ten
pattern. They will ask how often, how long, how strong, whether the waters have
gone and what colour they were, how the baby is moving, and how you are coping.
They will make the call with you. Go in immediately, without ringing first, for
anything on the emergency list, or if you feel a strong urge to push.

Go in earlier than the standard advice, and say why on the phone, if: this is
not your first baby and your last labour was quick; you live a long way from
the unit or the journey is unreliable; you have been asked to come in early for
a reason such as group B strep, a planned induction, diabetes, or a previous
caesarean; or you simply are not coping at home. That last one counts. It is a
legitimate reason and you do not have to dress it up.

Things that on their own are **not** a reason to go in: a show, a single
tightening that woke you up, a due date that has passed, or having "a feeling".
Things that are always worth a phone call even when you think you are being
silly: everything in the first section.

## Packing: three bags, not one

Pack from around 36 weeks. Three separate bags beat one enormous one, because
at three in the morning nobody wants to excavate a suitcase for lip balm.

**The labour bag** — the only one that comes into the room at first. Maternity
notes and ID. Phone plus a charging cable at least two metres long, because
sockets are never where the bed is. Lip balm; hospital air is dry and gas and
air dries the mouth further. A water bottle with a sports cap that works lying
down. Snacks that survive a hot bag. A hairband. A water spray or a flannel. A
TENS machine if you are using one, with spare batteries — put it on early, in
the latent phase, because it works on a build-up. A birth ball if the unit does
not supply one. Warm socks. Flip-flops for the shower. Something to play music
from.

**The postnatal bag** — for after. Two or three nightdresses or old pyjamas
that open at the front for feeding, all of which you should be happy never to
see again. Dark towels. Two packs of maternity pads — you will use more than
you expect. Big, cheap, high-waisted cotton knickers, several sizes up, which
matter enormously after a caesarean because the waistband sits above the wound.
Nursing bras if you plan to breastfeed. Toiletries, a hairbrush, and a going
home outfit that fits a body still around six months pregnant.

**The baby bag.** Nappies — more than you think, around a dozen for a short
stay. Cotton wool. Two or three sleepsuits and vests. A hat. A blanket. A
cardigan for the journey. Formula, ready-made in small bottles, if you are
formula feeding or want the option.

**Fitted in the car, this week:** the car seat. Not in the boot, still boxed —
actually fitted, with someone having practised the straps. Maternity units are
not allowed to let you leave without one, and a car park at midnight is a
terrible classroom. Learn the route, where to park, and which entrance is open
overnight, because the main doors usually are not.

**The partner packs a bag too**, and forgets to, every time: food, a change of
clothes, a toothbrush, painkillers, a phone charger of their own, and cash or a
card for parking. Labour can run through a night and a day. A support person
who has not eaten since lunchtime stops being support.

## Writing a birth plan that survives contact with reality

A birth plan is not a script. It is a one-page statement of preferences, and
its real value is the conversation you have while writing it — the moment you
discover you and your partner assumed opposite things about pain relief.

Keep it to a single side of paper. Staff will read one page. They will not read
five, and the plan that gets read is the plan that works.

Say what matters most, in order. Cover: who you want in the room; how you feel
about pain relief and in what order you would like to try things; whether you
want to move around and use water; how you feel about monitoring; who cuts the
cord and whether you want it left to stop pulsating; whether you want skin to
skin straight away; how you intend to feed; whether you consent to vitamin K
for the baby and by which route. Write down anything a stranger could not
guess: a previous traumatic birth, a phobia of needles, a religious
requirement, that you do not want to be offered pain relief unless you ask, or
that you very much do want to be offered it.

Write a second short paragraph headed **"if things change"**. Most plans go
unread precisely when they matter, because the situation changed and nobody
knows what you would have chosen. Say what you would want in an unplanned
caesarean: your partner present, the screen lowered so you can see the baby
born, skin to skin in theatre if it is possible, and who goes with the baby if
you are separated. Deciding this calmly in advance is worth more than every
other line on the page.

Frame it as preferences, not demands, and make sure your partner can recite it
without reading it. During labour they are the one who speaks it aloud.

Then bring the tool that makes any plan work: **BRAIN**. Whenever something is
suggested, anyone in the room can ask — what are the **B**enefits? What are the
**R**isks? What are the **A**lternatives? What does my **I**ntuition say? What
happens if we do **N**othing, or not yet? These questions are welcome. Asking
them is not obstruction, and no good midwife or obstetrician minds. In a
genuine emergency the honest answer to the last one is "we do not have time",
and that is your signal to trust them and go.

## What actually happens: the three stages

**First stage** is everything up to full dilatation, and it is the long one.
Latent phase at home, then established labour, contractions getting longer,
stronger and closer. Somewhere near the end comes **transition**, which is
short, intense, and has a recognisable emotional signature: shaking, feeling
sick, sudden fury, and above all announcing that she cannot do this. Transition
is the moment people say they want to go home, or ask for an epidural they were
adamant about refusing. It usually means the baby is nearly here. The partner's
job at transition is to hold the line calmly, not to negotiate, and above all
not to leave the room.

**Second stage** is pushing, and the sensation changes completely — from pain
that washes over you to a physical, involuntary urge, like needing the toilet.
It can take a few minutes or a couple of hours. There is often a "rest and be
thankful" lull between full dilatation and the urge arriving, and it is not a
sign anything has stalled. Towards the end the midwife may ask for small pants
rather than big pushes, to let the perineum stretch slowly. Listen to that
instruction over any instinct; it is the bit that protects you.

**Third stage** is delivering the placenta, and it happens while everyone is
looking at the baby. You will be offered an injection to speed it up and reduce
bleeding, or you can wait for it to come on its own; ask about both in advance
and put your preference on the page. Any stitching happens now, with local
anaesthetic. It is the part nobody mentions in advance, and being told it is
coming makes it much less alarming.

## The birth partner's job, hour by hour

Being useful during labour is a real skill, and almost none of it is intuitive.

**Early on:** run the contraction timer. Do the talking when you ring triage,
so she is not trying to describe a contraction during one. Keep the room dim
and the atmosphere boring. Feed her, and keep water within reach at all times.
Get her moving — walking, leaning, on the ball — because upright and mobile
does more than any breathing technique.

**In established labour:** counter-pressure on the lower back during a
contraction, hard, with the heel of your hand. Do not talk during a
contraction; talk between them. Repeat what the midwife says in your own voice,
because a familiar voice lands when a stranger's does not. Guard the room:
lights, noise, temperature, and who comes in. Be the one who knows the plan and
says it out loud, and ask the BRAIN questions when something is proposed.

**At transition:** stay. Say some version of "you are doing it, you are nearly
there" and keep saying it. Do not take anything said to you personally, and do
not go and find a coffee.

**After the birth:** take the photographs nobody thinks about. Do skin to skin
yourself; it is not a consolation prize, and it settles the baby. Take over
nappies from the very first one. Be the gatekeeper for visitors, including your
own family, which is harder and matters more. And keep an eye on two things
nobody has appointed you to watch: how much she is bleeding, and how she is
doing in herself.

**In the first fortnight:** register the birth — within 42 days in England and
Wales. Take your paternity leave. Do the night nappy changes and the bottle
washing. Answer the phone. Say no on her behalf.

## The first hour and the first days

The hour after birth is worth protecting. Skin to skin, straight away and
uninterrupted, regulates the baby's temperature, heart rate and blood sugar,
and it works just as well with the non-birthing parent when it has to. Ask for
the cord to be left to stop pulsating if that is your preference — an extra
minute or two moves a meaningful amount of blood to the baby. The first feed
usually happens in this hour, often with a lot of fumbling. Weighing, the
vitamin K injection and the newborn check can all wait a while, and if you ask,
they usually will.

Then the days that follow, which nobody photographs.

**Feeding** is a skill both of you are learning at once, and it is normal for
it to be hard for a fortnight. Newborns feed eight to twelve times in twenty-
four hours, often clustered into a relentless evening block. Cluster feeding is
normal and is not evidence of insufficient milk. Your milk "coming in" around
day three arrives with engorgement and, frequently, tears. If feeding hurts
beyond the first few seconds of a latch, that is a signal to get help — from
your midwife, a breastfeeding counsellor, or the National Breastfeeding
Helpline on 0300 100 0212 — rather than something to endure.

**Nappies are the objective measure**, which makes them the antidote to
worrying about whether the baby is getting enough. One or two wet nappies on
day one, building to at least six heavy wet ones a day from day five onwards.
Stools go from black, tarry meconium through dark green to a yellow, seedy
mustard by around day five. Babies commonly lose up to a tenth of their birth
weight in the first days and are back to birth weight by about two weeks.

**Sleep** is fragmented and there is no fixing that in the early weeks. Divide
the night into shifts so each of you gets one unbroken block, rather than both
of you waking for everything. For safer sleep: on the back, in a clear flat
cot, in your room for the first six months, and never on a sofa or armchair
with the baby.

**Visitors** will want to come immediately. You are allowed to make them wait.
The ones worth having bring food and leave within the hour.

**Who checks on you:** the midwife visits in the first days, the blood spot
test happens around day five, and a health visitor takes over at around ten
days to two weeks.

## Recovery, and the part that gets skipped

Postnatal recovery is treated as an afterthought by nearly everyone. It should
not be.

**Bleeding**, called lochia, is heaviest and red for the first three or four
days, then gradually browns and lightens over two to six weeks. It should
trend downwards. Sudden bright red bleeding after it had settled, or the
soaking-a-pad-an-hour threshold from the first section, means ring now.

**Stitches and the perineum:** keep the area clean and dry, pour warm water
while you wee if it stings, use a cushion, and take the pain relief you are
offered rather than being stoic about it. Increasing pain after day three,
rather than decreasing, is not normal and needs looking at.

**After a caesarean** you are recovering from major abdominal surgery. No
lifting anything heavier than the baby, no driving until you can perform an
emergency stop without hesitating and your insurer agrees, and support the
wound with a hand or a pillow when you cough or laugh. Redness spreading from
the wound, discharge, or a fever means it needs seeing.

**The pelvic floor** matters for decades and gets ten minutes of attention.
Start the exercises as soon as you comfortably can. Leaking urine is common and
is not something you have to accept permanently; ask about a referral to a
women's health physiotherapist.

**Mood.** The "baby blues" arrive around day three to five, with tearfulness
and being overwhelmed, and lift within a fortnight. What is not baby blues:
low mood that persists beyond two weeks, an inability to sleep even when the
baby does, no enjoyment in anything, or frightening intrusive thoughts.
Postnatal depression affects more than one in ten new mothers, and fathers and
partners get it too. It responds well to treatment, and treatment starts with
telling your GP or health visitor the truth rather than the polite version.
Thoughts of harming yourself or the baby need help the same day.

**The six-week check** with your GP is for you, not only the baby. Bring a
written list; you will forget otherwise. Contraception matters sooner than
people expect, since fertility can return before periods do, and there is no
schedule for resuming sex beyond your own comfort.

The recurring theme of this whole guide: you are the expert on what is normal
for you, and a phone call is free. When something has changed, ring. Nobody
will mind.
